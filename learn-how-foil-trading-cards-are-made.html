How Foil Trading Cards Are Made

“Foil” means a few different things depending on who's making the card. Here's specifically how it works here.

Printed holographic vinyl, not foil stamping

A lot of people picture foil stamping when they hear "foil card" — a metallic foil pressed onto paper with heat and a die. That's not what this is. Foil cards here print directly onto holographic vinyl using the same pigment-ink process as everything else, so the art itself sits on a material that's already holographic, rather than having a separate foil layer applied after the fact.

Why vinyl instead of paper for foil cards specifically

Holographic vinyl brings two things paper can't: it's genuinely waterproof, and it holds up to handling in a way a foil-stamped paper card generally doesn't. The trade-off is that vinyl alone is thin and flexible — nowhere near stiff enough to feel like a real card — which is why foil fronts always get mounted to a 199gsm core afterward. That step is what turns a flexible printed sheet into something with the weight and snap of a real card.

The rest of the process is the same as any other card

Once the front is printed, it goes through the same steps as a standard card — full 24-hour cure, lamination and sealing on the back, hand die-cutting, immediate sleeving. Foil doesn't skip any of the steps that make a card hold up; it just changes what the front is made of.

When foil is the right call

Foil makes the most sense for alt-art or holo recreations, or any custom piece where the shine and shift-in-light is part of the point. For something meant to read cleanly at the table without glare, standard is usually the better fit — see Standard vs Foil Proxy Cards for that comparison in full.
